A stranger arrives in a van, parks in your driveway and starts cleaning your car. It is reasonable to wonder whether that person is legitimate, what they will do with the water, whether they need your electricity and what happens if something goes wrong. Most mobile detailers are honest working professionals, and the format is convenient, but the questions are good ones. This guide covers what to ask, what a good answer sounds like and the red flags that suggest you should look elsewhere.

We are a mobile service ourselves, so we have an interest here. We have tried to write this the way we would want a customer to read it: as a checklist you can use on us or anyone else.

## Water: what do they need, and where does it go?

**What they need.** Many mobile detailers carry their own water. Some rely on your outdoor tap. Ask which, because it affects whether you need to provide anything. If you have no tap, say so in advance, and a good detailer will plan around it or tell you plainly that it is a problem. Methods that use very little water also exist, and they matter in drought years and in apartment complexes.

**Where it goes.** This is the more important question. In the Sacramento area, discharging wash water, soap or cleaning agents into a storm drain is not permitted. The City of Sacramento's published guidance for commercial car washing says so directly, and its stated aim is that only rain goes down storm drains, because those drains lead to local waterways without treatment. That guidance is written for commercial washing, so how it applies to a mobile operator is a question of interpretation, and we are not offering legal advice. West Sacramento publishes its own guidance for mobile cleaning, which advises directing wash water to vegetation or gravel, or to the sanitary sewer where possible.

A professional answer to "where does your wash water go?" mentions low-water methods, containing runoff, or keeping it off the street. A red-flag answer is a shrug, or a hose pointed at the gutter.

If you own or manage property, note that guidance in other California areas says property owners can be affected by what a contractor discharges on their site. That is another reason to ask.

## Power: do they need your electricity?

Some equipment, like a vacuum or a polisher, needs power. Many detailers bring a generator or a battery pack, and some rely on a customer outlet. Ask. If you have no outdoor outlet, tell them in advance. A good detailer plans for that. We ask customers to tell us when they book if they have no tap or outlet, so that we can plan the visit. Also ask whether the equipment is quiet enough for your neighbors and your building's rules.

## Insurance: what covers your car?

This is the question most people forget. Accidents are rare, but they happen: a polisher slips, a chemical stains a seat, a door gets bumped. Ask whether the detailer carries liability insurance, and whether they carry garage-keepers coverage, which relates to customer vehicles in their care. Industry guidance often cites general liability coverage as standard and a certificate of insurance as something many property managers and dealers ask for, though the specifics vary. A legitimate business will answer without hesitation and can usually provide a certificate on request. If you hear defensiveness, think twice.

Property managers and dealerships commonly require proof of insurance before they allow work on site. Our [dealership recon page](/services/dealership-recon) explains how that works for lots.

## Licensing: what to ask

California has no state license specifically for car detailing, and general guidance points to local business licenses and water-discharge compliance as the main requirements. Which city licenses apply to a mobile operator serving several cities is something we recommend checking directly with each city, and that is true for any detailer, not just us. A simple question, "Are you a registered business?", is reasonable and a legitimate operator will not mind it. Dent repair work may involve different rules, which is a topic a business owner should check with an advisor.

## Reputation: how to check

- **Look for real reviews.** Look at the number, the dates and the detail. A cluster of identical five-star reviews posted in a few days is a warning sign. Specific, varied reviews with photos are more credible.
- **Ask for before-and-after photos** of recent jobs. Be wary of photos that look like stock images.
- **Check how they communicate.** A professional answers questions clearly, confirms the scope and the time window and gives you a written estimate.
- **Look for a real business presence.** A website, a phone number, consistent branding and a clear service area are good signs. Service-area businesses legitimately do not publish a street address, since there is no shop to visit.

## Pricing red flags

- A price far below everyone else, with no explanation of what is included.
- A price that is "firm" before anyone has seen the car.
- Pressure to pay in full in advance, in cash, with no receipt.
- Unwillingness to describe what is included in a detail.

A fair process looks like ours: you send photos, you get an estimate range, the price is confirmed on inspection and you pay after seeing the result. See [how long mobile detailing takes](/blog/how-long-does-mobile-detailing-take) for what to expect on the day.

## Questions you can copy and paste

<Callout title="A quick vetting list">
1. Do you bring your own water and power, or do you need mine?
2. Where does your wash water go?
3. Do you carry liability insurance, and can I see a certificate?
4. Are you a registered business in this area?
5. What exactly is included in this service, and what is not?
6. How long will it take, and what could change that?
7. When do I pay, and what if I am not happy?
</Callout>
